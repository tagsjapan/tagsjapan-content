# Karoshi: When Work Becomes a Cause of Death

Late at night in a Japanese office district, the most telling light is the one that does not go out. A few floors up, fluorescent panels still glow over rows of desks. In the street below, the last trains are close, and the hour is late enough that “going home” feels like an operational decision rather than an automatic right. Japan has many words that capture social discipline, but few have carried as much weight as `Karoshi（過労死）`—death from overwork. It is a term that forces a prosperous, highly organized society to look at the cost of its competence.

Karoshi originally referred mainly to sudden death from cerebrovascular or cardiac conditions linked to long working hours and extreme workload. In recent government usage, the frame is wider. Japan often speaks of `過労死等（death and related harm from overwork）`, which includes not only death but serious illness, mental disorders caused by intense work stress, and suicides connected to work-related psychological harm. The expansion is not semantic fussiness; it is an admission that overwork is not a single medical event. It is a pressure system that can break bodies quickly, wear them down slowly, and push minds into collapse.

The term rose into public consciousness in the 1980s, when Japan’s corporate model—built on long hours, high loyalty expectations, and a division of labor that assumed someone would always stay late—was still treated as a national strength. When workers died, early narratives often framed it as individual health failure. Over time, families, lawyers, and journalists fought to rename those deaths as consequences of work itself. That renaming was a political act. It moved the story from private tragedy to public responsibility.

A decisive institutional shift came in 2014, when Japan enacted the `Act on Promotion of Preventive Measures against Karoshi and Other Overwork-Related Health Disorders（過労死等防止対策推進法）`. The law signaled that the state would no longer treat karoshi only as case-by-case compensation. It was now a problem to be prevented through continuous policy—research, education, consultation systems, and public reporting. The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW) began issuing an annual white paper on karoshi-related measures, turning what had been a shocking social keyword into a steady piece of governance language. That shift is itself revealing: Japan often responds to social pain by converting it into a system that can be monitored, summarized, and iterated.

Institutionalization, though, does not mean the problem has disappeared. Japan’s `働き方改革（work style reform）` agenda and overtime regulations have changed the official posture toward long hours, and many workplaces have reduced extreme schedules. Yet sectors facing chronic labor shortages—transport, healthcare and welfare, services—continue to rely on high workloads and emotional strain to keep operations running. The result is a distinctly modern kind of contradiction: karoshi is widely known, publicly condemned, and still produced by the practical demands of a high-pressure economy.

The numbers make that contradiction hard to romanticize. In a release dated 25 June 2025, MHLW reported that overwork-related compensation claims reached 4,810 cases, with 1,304 cases approved for payment; cases involving death or suicide totaled 159. The breakdown is equally important. Claims for brain and heart diseases were 1,030, with 241 approvals. Claims for mental disorders were 3,780, with 1,055 approvals. The implication is not subtle: in contemporary Japan, the largest share of the overwork problem is psychological. Karoshi is no longer only the dramatic image of collapse at a desk; it is also depression, burnout, and the long tunnel of stress disorders that can end in self-harm.

Industry patterns underline the shift. MHLW’s materials show mental-disorder claims and approvals concentrated in sectors such as medical care and welfare, while brain and heart disease remains severe in areas like transport, postal services, and construction. Japan’s overwork problem has spread beyond the classic white-collar myth of the salaryman and into the infrastructure of everyday life: logistics that must not fail, care work that cannot be postponed, customer-facing labor that absorbs anger and anxiety as part of the job. When labor is scarce and expectations remain high, the work expands to fill the gap.

Karoshi also exposes how hard it is to change a system through rules alone. Overtime caps and compliance frameworks matter, but they collide with deeper structures: staffing models, promotion incentives, the moral prestige of endurance, and the social pressure not to become a burden on colleagues. In many workplaces, the real decision is not “Is overwork illegal?” but “Who will absorb the work if I stop?” That question turns individual self-protection into a group problem, and it is one reason reforms often stall at the level of paperwork while pressure continues to flow through the organization.

For families, the struggle does not end with the death or breakdown. Recognition matters. Compensation processes can be lengthy, and the burden of proving that a death was “work-related” forces loved ones to translate grief into documentation. Many pursue recognition not only for money but for social truth: to prevent a death from being quietly filed as personal weakness. In this respect, karoshi is not only a labor issue. It is a battle over naming—over whether Japanese society will admit, in official language, that a system can kill.

Karoshi remains one of the sharpest concepts for understanding Japan because it sits at the seam between virtues and costs. Discipline, reliability, and organizational efficiency are real. So is the damage they can produce when endurance becomes a default expectation rather than an exceptional choice.
