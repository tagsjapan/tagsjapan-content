# Shinto: The Religion Japan Often Practices Without Announcing

On the first days of January, millions of people in Japan line up at shrines, ring bells, bow, clap, pray, buy lucky charms, and begin the year by passing through a sacred precinct. Many of them would hesitate if asked whether they belong to a religion in the strong doctrinal sense. Yet the actions are familiar, patterned, and meaningful. That is one of the best entry points into Shinto. It is a tradition often lived before it is defined.

Shinto is commonly described as Japan's indigenous religion, which is true as far as it goes. The difficulty is that Shinto does not fit the expectations many readers bring to the word "religion." It has no single founder, no one revealed scripture, and no universally binding creed that all adherents must learn before participating. Its center lies elsewhere: in `kami`, in shrine ritual, in purification, in seasonal festivals, in ties to place, and in the repeated marking of thresholds in ordinary life. The point is less belief recited aloud than order enacted through gesture.

That helps explain why the tradition can feel so natural to many Japanese even when they do not speak of themselves as deeply religious. A family may visit a shrine at New Year, mark a child's growth through Shichi-Go-San, ask for protection during pregnancy, hold a ground-purification rite before building a house, and keep a household altar without ever feeling that they have crossed into a heavily confessional world. Shinto has long thrived in that space between ritual familiarity and explicit doctrinal commitment. It asks for participation at charged moments rather than constant verbal declaration, and that rhythm suits the way many Japanese still encounter religion.

The tradition is also older and more mixed than nationalist simplifications usually admit. The Jinja Honcho description of Shinto begins with reverence toward nature, life force, ancestors, and the powers felt in mountains, seas, forests, and particular places. But the word "Shinto" itself only became necessary after Buddhism arrived in the 6th century, when a distinction had to be made between the local tradition and the imported one. For centuries thereafter, the two were not neatly separated. Shrines and temples shared space. Kami and buddhas were interpreted through one another. The history of Japanese religion is not a tale of clean boundaries. It is a long story of overlap.

That overlap was broken and rearranged in the modern era. During the Meiji period, the state reorganized shrine life and tied Shinto far more closely to emperor-centered national order. This was not simply a theological shift. It was administrative and political. Shrines became entangled with state ritual, public morality, and national integration. After 1945 the structure changed again. The state-backed framework was dismantled, and in 1946 Jinja Honcho was established to coordinate most shrines within a new religious-corporate and civil framework. Modern Shinto, then, is not ancient continuity untouched by history. It is continuity after repeated restructuring.

Numbers from the Agency for Cultural Affairs make the scale visible even if they do not settle the question of personal belief. The 2025 religious yearbook reports about 84,014 Shinto-affiliated religious corporations and roughly 86,359,612 adherents as of December 31 in Reiwa 6, while also noting that religious bodies define adherents in different ways. Those numbers are useful less as proof of doctrinal allegiance than as evidence of institutional reach. Shrines remain woven into the country's social and ritual infrastructure. Few countries could sustain that many shrine-linked bodies if the tradition had become merely decorative. Whatever else has changed, the network still works.

Shinto survives because it is concrete.

It survives in the rinse of water at the purification basin, in the shrine festival that still binds a neighborhood together, in the portable mikoshi carried through streets, in the sense that a place has a guardian presence, in the understanding that impurity and renewal are not only personal matters but communal ones. This is where Shinto becomes especially revealing of Japan. It shows a society in which religion often works through seasonal rhythm, local belonging, and bodily practice rather than through public declaration alone.

That does not make Shinto innocent of politics. Questions surrounding Yasukuni Shrine, state ceremony, wartime memory, and the constitutional boundary between religion and public authority continue to give the tradition a sharper edge. One cannot understand modern Japan by seeing Shinto only as a gentle folklore of festivals and blessings. It has also served as a language of nationhood, legitimacy, and contested historical memory. The same shrine world that blesses a child can also reopen arguments about the state and the past.

Shinto therefore matters not because every Japanese person consciously "believes" in the same way, but because the tradition still helps organize how space is marked, how seasons are felt, how communities gather, and how continuity is imagined. A shrine visit at New Year, a local festival in summer, a few claps before a prayer spoken under one's breath: these are small scenes. Taken together, they show how deeply Shinto remains embedded in the Japanese sense of life lived among powers that are near, local, and rarely entirely abstract.
