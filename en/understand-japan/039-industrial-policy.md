# Industrial Policy: How Japan Learned to Plan Through Markets

There is a familiar story in modern capitalism that says governments should set the rules, companies should compete, and the market should decide which industries deserve to live. Japan never entirely believed that story in its pure form. From the postwar years onward, the state repeatedly tried to guide, accelerate, protect, or redirect industry through a dense mix of regulation, finance, standards, tax measures, and administrative coordination. The label for that practice is `Industrial Policy`, and in Japan it has never meant just subsidies. It has meant the active shaping of economic structure.

The classical phase belongs to the postwar decades, when the Ministry of International Trade and Industry, or `MITI`, became famous for its role in coordinating technology imports, foreign-exchange allocation, and strategic support for sectors such as steel, automobiles, machinery, and electronics. The goal was not merely to let firms grow, but to move the country's industrial base toward higher value, stronger competitiveness, and greater national resilience. In a society rebuilding from war and facing intense international competition, industrial policy offered a way to compress development.

That model changed, but it did not vanish. After the oil shocks of the 1970s, Japan shifted attention toward energy efficiency, higher-value production, and technology-intensive sectors. After the bubble collapse and the more liberal climate of the 1990s, industrial policy was often discussed less as direct state steering and more as competitiveness, innovation, restructuring, and reform. Yet even when the vocabulary softened, the underlying habit remained: Japanese governments kept returning to the question of how public power might push the industrial base toward a desired future rather than merely observe the outcome.

The state did not treat the economy as weather.

The chronology makes the continuity visible. `MITI` was created in 1949, just as Japan was constructing the institutional tools of postwar recovery. After the 1973 oil shock, industrial policy shifted toward energy efficiency and higher-value production rather than simple quantitative expansion. In the 1990s, when globalization and stagnation weakened the prestige of older bureaucratic guidance, the policy did not disappear; it changed instruments. By the 2020s, the argument had returned with new urgency around semiconductors, batteries, green investment, and supply-chain resilience. The names changed from heavy industry to `GX`, `DX`, and economic security, but the state was still trying to answer the same underlying question: which productive capacities are too important to leave entirely to short-term market sorting?

That habit remains visible in today's policy architecture. The Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry still sits at the center of industrial strategy, now through tools such as the Industrial Competitiveness Enhancement Act, green-growth programs, semiconductor support, and supply-chain resilience measures. Current policy blends old and new concerns. The old concern is productivity and industrial strength. The new concerns include decarbonization, economic security, digital infrastructure, and the geopolitical vulnerability of key technologies. Industrial policy in Japan no longer looks like simple catch-up industrialization. It looks more like state-guided adaptation under pressure.

The appeal is obvious. Supporters argue that no advanced economy can leave semiconductors, batteries, carbon transition, and strategic manufacturing entirely to short-term market signals. In a world of U.S.-China rivalry, climate commitments, and supply-chain disruption, doing nothing is also a policy. Japan's recent strategies reflect that awareness. The government has tried to attract investment, stabilize domestic production capacity, and keep core industries from hollowing out. Industrial policy promises that the country can still choose its economic direction rather than simply endure it.

The criticism is equally familiar. States can back the wrong sectors, reward incumbents, or pour money into politically protected industries that are good at lobbying and poor at renewal. Japan's history supplies examples of both successful coordination and sluggish protection. The real issue is not whether industrial policy exists, but how clearly its goals are defined, how honestly outcomes are measured, and how far public intervention serves transformation rather than delay.

That is why industrial policy remains a key to understanding Japan. It reveals a governing style that has long preferred coordination to passivity and strategic patience to market fatalism. It also shows how Japan understands vulnerability. Energy dependence, technological rivalry, and demographic pressure all encourage the state to keep shaping the industrial map rather than leaving it to chance. The older partnership among bureaucrats, firms, banks, and law has changed form, but it has not become irrelevant.

In Japan, industrial policy is not an eccentric exception to capitalism. It is one of the country's recurring answers to the question of how an exposed nation keeps enough productive strength to remain wealthy, stable, and strategically self-respecting. That is also why each new industrial push arrives with echoes of older debates. Are public funds buying time for genuine adaptation, or merely protecting politically useful incumbents from discipline? Those questions followed `MITI` in the 1950s and 1970s and still follow `METI` in the 2020s. In Japan, strategy tends to arrive through ministries, law, finance, and administrative guidance rather than through rhetoric alone. That continuity is itself part of the story. It keeps strategic argument alive inside government and keeps industrial priorities open to revision over time and circumstance, repeatedly, still.
