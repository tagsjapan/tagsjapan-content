# SMEs: The Economy Japan Can’t Replace

In a side street far from any corporate headquarters, a small workshop opens its shutters and turns on fluorescent lights. Someone checks a delivery slip, someone else warms up a machine, and a phone call comes in from a customer who expects the same part, the same finish, and the same deadline as last time. Scenes like this rarely make business pages, yet they are the daily operating system of Japan’s economy. When Japanese policymakers talk about “SMEs,” they are not talking about charm or scrappiness. They are talking about the layer of firms that keeps production networks, local services, and employment from collapsing into a handful of giants.

The first thing to understand is that “Small and Medium-sized Enterprises (SMEs)” in Japan is not a fuzzy description. It is a legal and administrative category with thresholds that vary by industry. The Small and Medium Enterprise Agency explains that, for manufacturing and similar sectors, an SME is generally defined as having capital of 300 million yen or less or 300 or fewer regular employees. Wholesale, services, and retail have their own ceilings—100 million yen or 100 employees for wholesale, 50 million yen or 100 employees for services, and 50 million yen or 50 employees for retail. Those lines may look technical, but they decide who is eligible for particular forms of financing, support programs, and policy attention.

Japan built this category into its postwar development story. During reconstruction and high growth, large firms expanded global reach and brand power while relying on layers of smaller suppliers and subcontractors. In 1963, the Small and Medium-sized Enterprise Basic Act gave SMEs an explicit place in national policy, treating them as a foundation for stability in employment and local economies rather than as a leftover from modernization. A major revision in 1999 shifted the policy language further toward autonomy, innovation, and diverse management styles—an attempt to move the SME story beyond protection toward capability.

That policy attention is not sentimental. The 2025 Small and Medium Enterprise White Paper summary states that SMEs and small-scale businesses account for roughly 70% of employment in Japan. This is the statistic that changes the emotional register. It means that a large portion of working life—wages, career paths, and the durability of local communities—depends on firms that often have thin margins, limited bargaining power, and a high exposure to shocks. If a country’s employment is built on small firms, then “industrial policy” is never only about technology; it is also about whether ordinary workplaces can survive the next cost increase.

In the 2020s, that survival problem has become more visible. The White Paper points to a tough mix: a weaker yen, persistent price increases, the return of an interest-rate environment that many managers have not had to navigate for decades, and a structural labor shortage. Even when headline wage settlements rise—Japan’s spring wage negotiations in 2024 drew attention precisely because the increases looked like something from another era—the gap between what large firms can pay and what smaller firms can sustain tends to widen. The White Paper summary also notes that many SMEs’ labor share is already near 80%, leaving little room to raise pay further without a way to raise prices, upgrade productivity, or change what the firm sells.

This is where the “SME” label becomes a map of power. Many small firms sit inside supply chains where they cannot easily pass costs upward. Others sell to local customers who resist price hikes. That creates a blunt contradiction: society needs SMEs to keep towns functioning and factories running, yet the market mechanisms around them often punish the very stability people want. Digitalization, equipment investment, and management reform are repeatedly named as answers, but they require money and people—the two things SMEs struggle to secure.

Japan’s institutions try to bridge that gap. The state maintains an architecture of SME support that includes policy finance and programs designed for small and medium-sized firms; the Japan Finance Corporation explicitly frames SME and small business support as part of its role. Organizations such as the SME Support, Japan (SMRJ/JASMEC) provide clear explanations of the definition itself, precisely because eligibility has consequences. In other words, the SME category is not only descriptive; it is a gateway into a system that tries to keep local business failure from turning into social failure.

Yet the category also hides diversity. Some SMEs are high-skill manufacturers with niche global customers. Others are family-run service businesses whose biggest challenge is succession. Some are innovative, others are stuck, and many are both at once—skilled at what they have always done, uncertain about what comes next. When policymakers use the term “SME,” they are compressing a sprawling reality into something that can be governed.

If you want a quick way to see the shape of Japan’s economy, watch what happens when an SME can’t hire, can’t raise prices, and can’t find someone to take over the business. The problem is not a single firm’s mismanagement. It is the question of whether the country’s employment base—roughly 70% of it—can keep operating under new cost structures. Japan’s SMEs are often described as the economy’s “base,” but the better metaphor is infrastructure: quietly taken for granted, hard to rebuild once it cracks, and impossible to replace with slogans.
