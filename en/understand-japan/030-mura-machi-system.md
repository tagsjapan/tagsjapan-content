# Town and Village System: The Small Units That Made Japan Governable

Plenty of Japanese people now live in cities, work in metropolitan corridors, and think in terms of prefectures or train lines. Even so, the words `town` and `village` remain stubbornly alive in local government names, public offices, school districts, and community identity. A village office may serve only a few thousand residents; a town sign may stand beside a road far from any major station; an old municipal name may survive in conversation long after a merger. These small units are the late echo of a much larger institutional story: the modern `Town and Village System`.

In this entry, the term refers to `chonsonsei`, the `town and village system` established as part of Japan's modern local-government reforms in the late nineteenth century. It developed alongside the city system and provided the basic framework for organizing non-city local communities as public bodies. The subject is not village custom in the folkloric sense. It is the legal and administrative structure that turned settlements into units the modern state could recognize, tax, represent, and govern.

The crucial turning point came in the Meiji period. Around 1888 and 1889, Japan put in place the city system and the town and village system as part of the broader construction of modern local administration. That process did more than tidy a map. It translated scattered communities into a national order. Towns and villages gained defined status, local assemblies, executive authority, and a place inside a larger hierarchy linking the center, prefectures, and the local base. Japan's modern state was built from the top down in many respects, but it also had to be built from the bottom up, by deciding what counted as a governable local unit.

Modern Japan was measured, in part, through its smallest public bodies.

That legacy survives because municipalities are where public life becomes concrete. Towns and villages, later joined and sometimes overshadowed by cities, have handled schools, records, local infrastructure, welfare functions, ordinances, and the daily interface between state and resident. Even where reforms have changed the legal environment, the basic idea has remained: local government in Japan is not only something done at the national or prefectural level. It is also anchored in units close enough to the ground for people to know the office, the hall, and the people who run them.

Yet the system has never been frozen. Municipal mergers repeatedly changed the map, and the great `Heisei mergers` after 1999 transformed it dramatically. Japan moved from more than 3,200 municipalities at the end of the 1990s to fewer than 1,800 by 2010, as the state encouraged consolidation for reasons of fiscal efficiency and administrative capacity. Many towns and villages disappeared into larger cities. Others survived and kept their names. The reform solved some problems of scale while creating a new unease: a community can remain socially vivid even after its administrative form has been folded away.

The persistence of the old scale can be seen where mergers have already happened. A former village name may remain on bus stops, branch offices, school events, agricultural cooperatives, and festival banners long after the legal municipality has been absorbed into a larger city. Residents keep using it because it still marks the zone within which people know the land, the flood history, the family names, and the route children take to school. The town and village system created more than local offices. It created a public habit of recognizing community at a scale small enough to remember personally and large enough to govern.

That tension between efficiency and representation lies at the heart of the town and village story. Larger municipalities may manage services more easily in an aging, shrinking society. Smaller units can preserve a sharper sense of voice and local knowledge. Japan has tried to hold both concerns together, with mixed success. Some residents accepted mergers as necessary. Others worried that schools, branch offices, and public attention would drift farther away once the old unit was gone. The argument was never only about paperwork. It was about the political size of a community.

Understanding the town and village system helps explain why local Japan still often speaks in a scale smaller than the prefecture. Agricultural cooperatives, neighborhood associations, school catchment areas, disaster response habits, and local memory all continue to work through units that feel intimate enough to be lived rather than merely administered. Even when a legal town has vanished, the older local name may still carry more meaning than the larger municipality that absorbed it. That is why former town halls often remain emotionally central after consolidation. People may travel to a larger city office for paperwork and still say they belong to the older place, because the scale of memory and the scale of administration have drifted apart.

That is why the plain sign on a village office matters. It points to a long history in which the Japanese state learned to govern by defining and redefining its smallest public units. Towns and villages may seem modest, but they were part of the machinery through which modern Japan became legible to itself, and remembered itself.
