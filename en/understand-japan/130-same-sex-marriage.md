# Same-Sex Marriage: Japan’s Debate About the Register

Imagine a couple at a municipal counter with a marriage notification form filled in, witnesses signed, dates neatly written. In Japan, that sheet of paper is normally the legal start of marriage. For same‑sex couples, it becomes something else: a document that exposes the boundary of recognition. The question is not whether they can hold a ceremony, or whether friends will treat them as family. The question is whether the state will accept the form and let their relationship enter the country’s official architecture of kinship.

`Same-Sex Marriage（同性婚姻）` in Japan is therefore not primarily a debate about romance or lifestyle. It is a debate about institutional entry. Legal marriage is created through the acceptance of a `kon’in todoke（婚姻届）` and its recording in the `koseki（戸籍）`, the family register. The Ministry of Justice frames the mechanism through statutes—Civil Code Article `739` and the Family Register Act Article `74`—which tells you how non‑optional the pathway is. In Japan’s legal design, a relationship becomes a marriage when it becomes legible to the state.

It sounds like paperwork because it is. Japan’s marriage system is not built around a ceremony; it is built around a legal switch at the municipal counter. For couples kept outside the switch, everything else remains contingent.

That is why same‑sex marriage cannot be solved by goodwill alone. Partnership can be socially real and practically stable, and it can still remain legally partial if it cannot pass through the same door as an opposite‑sex couple. The `koseki（戸籍）` does not simply note that two adults live together; it certifies family relationships as public facts that other institutions rely on. Marriage is one of the register’s major status‑changing events. When the marriage door is closed, the consequences ripple: not only symbolic exclusion, but a missing “spouse” category that many areas of law and administration treat as a default unit.

Local government policy has tried to build a bridge across that gap. Since the 2010s, many municipalities have introduced `Partnership System（パートナーシップ制度）` programs that issue certificates recognizing same‑sex couples as partners for certain practical purposes. These programs can matter a great deal in daily life—housing applications, hospital visitation norms, workplace benefits, the ability to explain a relationship without improvising. Yet they are not marriage. They typically do not automatically produce the full bundle of legal effects that follow from marriage registration: inheritance defaults, spouse status across statutes, and a stable recognition that travels with a couple across jurisdictions and institutions.

The recent story of same‑sex marriage in Japan is therefore a story of pressure moving through the system in stages: local recognition, administrative interpretation, and judicial review, with national legislation lagging behind. A Supreme Court decision on `March 26, 2024` (in a case involving crime‑victim benefits) recognized that same‑sex partners may fall within statutory language protecting relationships “equivalent to a de facto marriage.” The decision did not create marriage, but it mattered for a different reason: it acknowledged, at the highest judicial level, that certain legal protections cannot be reserved only for opposite‑sex couples when the underlying relationship functions as a family. In `2025`, the Ministry of Justice published an organized list of provisions under its jurisdiction that can be interpreted as potentially including same‑sex partners under similar “de facto marriage” language. This is a distinctly Japanese form of change: not a sweeping new family code, but a set of official interpretations that carve out partial recognition while leaving the core institution untouched.

Then the courts began to press harder on the core itself. The Osaka High Court decision of `March 25, 2025` concluded that the civil‑law and family‑register framework that excludes same‑sex marriage violates Japan’s Constitution—specifically Article `14(1)` on equality under the law and Article `24(2)` on the principles governing marriage and family. The importance of such a decision is not that it instantly rewrites the Civil Code. It does not. Its importance is that it reframes the political question. The issue becomes less “Should we modernize?” and more “How long can the present exclusion remain legally defensible?”

The current moment in Japan feels like an unstable equilibrium. Local partnership systems expand visibility. Administrative interpretation expands limited legal effects. Judicial decisions raise constitutional stakes. Yet couples still cannot enter the central mechanism that makes “spouse” a portable legal fact: marriage registration and `koseki（戸籍）` entry. A relationship can be recognized in one setting and treated as irrelevant in another. That unevenness is not accidental; it is the shape of a system trying to absorb social change without rewriting its foundational categories.

Any full legalization would also be more than a single symbolic amendment. Japan’s marriage system is threaded through the Civil Code, the Family Register Act, and the administrative practices that connect names, parent‑child relationships, and household registration to everyday documentation. A reform that opens the marriage door would have to decide, in concrete terms, how the register records a family, how related statutes read the category of “spouse,” and how the state handles the downstream questions—some technical, some intensely political—that follow from recognizing a new kind of legal couple. That complexity is one reason Japan’s shifts often begin with partial measures and court‑driven pressure rather than immediate comprehensive redesign.

So “same‑sex marriage” in Japan is a debate about the register because the register is where Japan stores family reality. The municipal counter and the stamped form are not minor details; they are the country’s definition of entry. Until the law changes, the partnership certificate remains a bridge that does not reach the far bank. And until the register changes, Japan’s family recognition will continue to carry a visible seam—one that tells you as much about the state’s governing style as it does about love.
