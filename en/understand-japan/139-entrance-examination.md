# Entrance Examination: Japan’s Sorting Machine, Rebuilt Without Being Replaced

In Japan, the entrance examination season has a recognizable atmosphere. Winter trains carry students with clear folders and quiet faces. School corridors fill with practice sheets. Families learn to read time in a new way: not months, but test dates, application windows, and the last stretch before a result arrives. The ritual can look like a single event. In practice it is an institution—one of the country’s most durable devices for distributing opportunity and anxiety.

`Entrance Examination（入学試験）` in Japan is not one test paper. It is a selection system, especially in high school and university admissions, that decides who enters which school and by what route. In the university context, it is built from multiple components: the `Common Test for University Admissions（大学入学共通テスト）` and several major selection tracks, including `General Selection（一般選抜）`, `School Recommendation Selection（学校推薦型選抜）`, and `Comprehensive Selection（総合型選抜）`. The key point is not that Japan has become “less exam‑driven.” The point is that competition has been redistributed across more formats—scores, school records, interviews, essays, and self‑narration.

Japan’s older image of admissions is captured by a phrase: `juken sensō（受験戦争）`, “exam war.” The phrase was never only about difficulty. It was about social legitimacy. A single, standardized test could be framed as fair: everyone sits the same exam, and the ranking looks clean. That logic helped stabilize a postwar narrative that effort could override background, and it created the ecosystem that grew around it—cram schools, school‑based tracking, and a culture that treats admission as a moral verdict on discipline.

What is most distinctive today is not the disappearance of that logic, but its modification. MEXT continues to coordinate university admissions through the annual `Implementation Guidelines for University Admissions（大学入学者選抜実施要項）`, an explicit signal that admissions is a national governance concern, not only a university choice. The Common Test, administered by the National Center for University Entrance Examinations, provides a nationwide reference scale. MEXT’s `2026` admissions overview shows how far multi‑track selection has spread. `Comprehensive Selection` was implemented at `161` of `179` national and public universities and in `577` of `640` faculties. `School Recommendation Selection` was implemented at `173` universities and `521` faculties. This is not a side channel. It is the new normal. Even the procedures are modernizing: the Common Test began `Web application（Web出願）` for `FY2026`, turning a classic paper-based ritual into an online workflow. The season also stretches: recommendation and comprehensive routes can open months before the traditional winter exams, and students increasingly manage multiple application strategies rather than betting everything on a single day.

The entrance exam is also a calendar that reorganizes daily life.

The reform language behind the multi‑track shift is captured in MEXT’s emphasis on `the three elements of academic ability（学力の3要素）`: knowledge and skills, thinking/judgment/expression, and motivation or the capacity for proactive learning. In theory, this is a corrective to the “single-score” model. Universities are encouraged to evaluate more than test technique. That is why interviews, essays, activity records, and statements of purpose have grown in importance, especially in comprehensive selection. The system is trying to claim a broader definition of merit while keeping the legitimacy that standardized testing once supplied almost automatically.

This produces a characteristic contradiction. Multi‑dimensional evaluation can reduce the sense that one exam decides a life. It can also widen the advantage of those with better guidance and resources. Preparing for a test is hard, but it is legible: you can buy books, solve problems, and track progress. Preparing a narrative, selecting achievements, writing a persuasive statement, and performing in an interview is a different kind of work—more dependent on coaching, school culture, and familiarity with the unspoken expectations of admissions committees. The stress does not disappear; it spreads. The competition moves earlier into daily school life and becomes harder to measure, which makes “fairness” feel less obvious even as the system claims to be more holistic.

Fairness therefore remains the system’s most sensitive nerve. MEXT maintains dedicated material on ensuring fairness in admissions, including scrutiny of medical-school admissions practices, because the legitimacy of the entire sorting machine depends on the belief that procedures are not rigged. When the process is trusted, the system can be endured as a harsh but meaningful competition. When the process is doubted, the system becomes not only stressful but socially corrosive, because it undermines the promise that effort and ability are being judged under shared rules.

To understand Japan, entrance examinations are an unusually powerful lens because they connect institutions that are often discussed separately: high schools, universities, families, cram‑school industries, and the state’s narrative of equal opportunity. Japan has not “abolished” the exam war. It has tried to renovate it—adding routes, adding formats, adding values—while keeping the machine running. The result is a system that is less like a single decisive battle and more like a long campaign, one that organizes adolescence as preparation and treats admission as the gate between ordinary life and a future with options. That is why reform rarely looks like simplification; it looks like an additional layer of evaluation built on top of an older layer that people still trust.
