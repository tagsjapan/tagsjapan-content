# Dementia: Japan’s Shift from Management to Coexistence

A person reaches the register and cannot remember the PIN. A bank clerk slows down, lowers their voice, and tries to keep the moment from becoming humiliating. A family notices the same question asked three times in ten minutes and then argues, quietly, about whether to “tell the doctor” or “wait and see.” In Japan, these scenes do not stay private for long. `Dementia（認知症）` has become one of the country’s central public issues not only because of medicine, but because a diagnosis forces a society to decide what it means to live together when memory and judgment change.

The medical baseline is clear. The World Health Organization describes dementia as a syndrome that affects memory, thinking, orientation, comprehension, calculation, learning capacity, language, and judgment—and it stresses that dementia is not a normal part of aging. Japan’s policy language adds another layer: dementia is treated as a question of welfare, rights, participation, and community capacity. The challenge is not only “what happens in the brain,” but what happens in daily life after the label arrives—whether the person can keep moving through familiar places, keep relationships, and keep some control over decisions.

In other words, dementia turns ordinary life into a social test.

Japan’s policy pivot over the last decade has been toward a new framing: from “problem management” to “coexistence.” A major step came with the 2019 `認知症施策推進大綱` (Dementia Policy Promotion Outline), which placed “prevention” and “living together” side by side. The more symbolic break arrived on January 1, 2024, when the `認知症基本法`—a Basic Act for promoting a coexistence society—took effect. Later that same year, on December 3, 2024, the Cabinet Secretariat published the first `認知症施策推進基本計画` (Basic Plan for promoting dementia measures), explicitly advancing what it calls `新しい認知症観`, a “new view of dementia” that emphasizes the person’s voice, social participation, and the idea that anyone can become an affected person.

That “new view” does not deny burden. It tries to change what the burden is allowed to mean. Under an older logic, dementia governance often looks like containment: early detection, keeping people from getting lost, reducing family exhaustion, managing risk. Japan still does those things. MHLW’s policy pages cover missing older adults, supporter programs, research, information on new medicines, and even disaster-related support. The difference is that the center of gravity is shifting. The state is now saying, in official language, that a person with dementia remains a person with preferences, relationships, and a place in the community—not only a safety problem to be managed.

Japan therefore invests heavily in “social infrastructure for understanding.” The `認知症サポーター` (dementia supporter) program is designed to train residents, students, and frontline service workers—people in shops, financial institutions, and local organizations—to respond with basic knowledge rather than fear. It is not an attempt to turn everyone into clinicians. It is an attempt to reduce stigma and prevent small confusions from turning into exclusion. When the state builds a mass training program around a disease concept, it is admitting that the community’s behavior is part of the outcome.

Rights and decision-making sit at the center of the new framing. MHLW has published guidance on decision-making support for people with dementia in daily and social life, reflecting a clear policy stance: cognitive decline does not automatically erase a person’s agency. The guidance exists because real life repeatedly creates dilemmas—medical consent, financial management, living arrangements—where “safety” can become an excuse to remove choice. Japan’s “coexistence” language pushes back against that reflex by insisting that support should include dialogue and respect, not only protection.

Clinical and technological change adds another layer of tension. Japan has been organizing information about new anti‑Aβ antibody medicines and related clinical guidance, feeding public hope that treatment breakthroughs will change the trajectory. Hope is real, but so are constraints: who is eligible, who pays, where specialist capacity concentrates, and how uneven access can widen regional and class differences. A society can adopt a “new view” of dementia and still reproduce old inequalities through the geography and cost of healthcare.

The hardest pressure remains scale. Japan’s welfare white paper materials include projections showing that the number of people living with dementia will remain high in the future, which means coexistence cannot be a boutique ideal. It has to be built into staffing, services, transport, and public spaces. This is where Japan’s approach becomes visibly Japanese: national laws and plans set direction, while local governments are expected to translate that direction into concrete programs. Dementia governance is therefore not only about individuals. It is about municipal capacity and the day-to-day coordination work that holds communities together.

You can see the system’s ambition in ordinary examples: a community program trains supporters; a municipality is pushed to produce a local plan; a family learns about services and rights; a workplace adjusts; a shop learns how to respond. None of this eliminates the pain of dementia. It tries to reduce the secondary damage—the isolation, the stigma, the quiet stripping away of dignity—by making “understanding” part of public policy rather than a private virtue. Japan’s dementia policy shift is ultimately an argument about what an aging society owes its members. If everyone is a potential future patient, then dementia cannot be handled only behind clinic doors. It has to be negotiated in the open: in language, in law, and in the routines of people who may never touch a medical chart but who decide, in a single encounter, whether someone still belongs.
