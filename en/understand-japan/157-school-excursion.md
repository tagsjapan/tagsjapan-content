# School Excursion: Japan’s Overnight Lesson in Group Life

On a station platform early in the morning, the class looks less like a group of individuals than a moving organism. Matching bags. Name tags. A teacher with a clipboard calling roll again and again, not because anyone is missing, but because counting is part of the ritual. Students line up, step onto the train in clusters, and try—half obediently, half excitedly—to keep their voices down. Before the first destination appears, the main work has already begun: learning how to move together without falling apart.

`School Excursion（修學旅行）` in Japan is not simply a “class trip.” It is a formally positioned school event, usually involving at least one overnight stay, and it belongs to the curriculum category known as `Special Activities（特別活動）`. That placement matters. It signals that the point is not only what students see, but what they practice: group living, discipline, pre- and post-learning, and the formation of shared memory. The Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology (MEXT) has even framed school excursions as meaningful educational activities, something to be carried out with care rather than treated as expendable entertainment.

The institution became possible only when two infrastructures matured at once: the modern school system and mass mobility. Once school was expected to teach social life as well as classroom knowledge, and once railways and lodging systems could handle large groups, “leaving the classroom to learn elsewhere” became administratively feasible. After 1945, as junior high and high school attendance expanded, school excursion culture spread beyond elite schools. It became a shared generational experience. The form stayed stable—teachers, schedules, group rooms, curfews—while the content evolved from “go see the classics” to theme-based learning that can include peace education, disaster learning, local industry study, and project-style inquiry.

What looks like a trip is, operationally, an exercise in managed freedom. Students plan parts of the schedule, but within tight boundaries of time, place, and rules. The group moves as a unit, yet small units inside it—班 (class groups), room groups, committee roles—do specific tasks. Teachers become managers of risk, fairness, and learning outcomes. In Japanese school culture, where routine is a teaching tool, the excursion works as a condensed version of everyday discipline: punctuality, coordination, not burdening others, and keeping the group readable.

Everyone remembers the roll call.

The numbers show that this is not a fading custom. The Japan School Trip Association’s 2024 survey reports that `93.8%` of responding junior high schools conducted domestic school excursions, with `4.3%` conducting them overseas; high schools reported `89.3%` domestic and `9.1%` overseas. Costs are also concrete and rising: in 2024, average expenses were reported as `73,805` yen for junior high and `114,623` yen for high school. This is where the institution becomes visibly political. When prices climb, the “shared experience” is harder to guarantee, and schools have to work harder to justify decisions that can expose household inequality.

The same survey data also reveals the characteristic Japanese compromise between collective control and limited autonomy. Class-based “independent action” (班別自主行動) remains common, with implementation rates above `87%` for both junior high (`87.7%`) and high school (`87.2%`). Students are expected to demonstrate initiative, but only in a frame where the school can still account for everyone’s location and safety. That design teaches a specific social skill: navigating freedom without breaking the group’s trust.

The content of excursions helps explain why certain places remain recurrent. Historic capitals such as Kyoto and Nara function as a physical curriculum for “what counts as Japanese culture.” Sites linked to war and memory—Hiroshima, Nagasaki, Okinawa—fit into peace education and the postwar national narrative. More recent trends add different layers: linking parts of the excursion to `総合的な学習の時間` (integrated studies), adopting SDGs themes, incorporating disaster-preparedness learning, or visiting workplaces and regional projects. MEXT’s own Q&A treats school excursions as part of a broader educational framework rather than a single-purpose outing, and schools increasingly frame them as projects with deliverables rather than as rituals justified by tradition alone.

That shift is also a response to pressure. In 2024, the association’s survey on “future forms” of school excursions found that `32.2%` of junior high schools and `34.5%` of high schools believed their excursions would be affected or change. The fault lines are familiar: cost burden, safety responsibility (especially after the pandemic), and the question of whether the excursion is drifting too far toward sightseeing and away from education. In the older model, the institution could lean on habit: everyone does it, so it must be good. In the newer model, schools are expected to explain purpose, manage risk with professional seriousness, and show that the learning is real.

`School Excursion（修學旅行）` is therefore a compact lesson in how Japan builds citizens. It uses movement to teach coordination, and it uses destinations to teach national memory. It is simultaneously a school timetable item, a rite of passage, and a small logistics operation that forces adults to balance ideals against budgets and safety. When the class returns to the station at the end—tired, carrying souvenirs, trying to stay in formation—the main product is not what was purchased or photographed. It is the experience of being a group that left together and came back together, which is exactly the kind of social competence Japanese education has long treated as teachable.
