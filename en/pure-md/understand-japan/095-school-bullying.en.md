# School Bullying: The Hurt That Must Be Recognized

The message appears after school, not during class. A class group chat falls silent when one student's name comes up. A photo is circulated with a caption meant to amuse everyone but the person in it. No teacher sees the exchange. No punch is thrown in the corridor. By bedtime the target already knows what has happened: tomorrow's classroom will feel narrower than yesterday's. In Japan, the word that gathers this kind of pain is `ijime`.

It is a broader word than "school violence."

Under the `2013` Act on the Promotion of Measures to Prevent Bullying, `ijime` means an act by a child or student against another child or student with whom there is a certain interpersonal relationship, causing psychological or physical suffering, including through the internet. The crucial point in official guidance is that the judgment should be made from the victim's standpoint. That shifts the focus away from obvious physical harm and toward exclusion, humiliation, rumor-spreading, persistent teasing, and online abuse. Japan's public language on bullying is built around injury that may be socially hidden even when it is emotionally unmistakable.

The urgency of the issue comes from a long and painful public history. Since the `1980s`, deaths by suicide and cases of severe distress linked to bullying have repeatedly forced schools, boards of education, and the wider society to confront the gap between what children experience and what institutions admit. For years the scandal was not only that children hurt each other. It was that adults failed to recognize the hurt until it hardened into tragedy. The `2013` law was a response to that history. Schools were no longer expected to rely on personal judgment alone. They were required to create policies, establish recognition and reporting procedures, and investigate serious incidents.

Those procedures changed daily school administration in ways that are easy to miss from outside. Every school is expected to have a basic policy on bullying. Boards of education must prepare for serious incidents. Once a case is suspected, adults are supposed to start from the possibility of harm rather than from the hope that the children will sort it out themselves. The bureaucratic paperwork can feel cold, but it exists because so many earlier cases became scandals only after institutions insisted for too long that nothing was really happening.

The official numbers are startling and deliberately difficult to read in a simple way. On `January 16, 2026`, the Ministry of Education released the results of the fiscal `2024` survey on student guidance issues. It recorded roughly `769,000` recognized bullying cases in elementary, lower secondary, upper secondary, and special-needs schools, along with `1,405` serious incidents, both the highest figures on record. The ministry has repeatedly argued that higher recognition numbers do not automatically mean schools are becoming worse. They may also mean staff are identifying problems earlier and treating low-level cases as bullying from the start. That interpretation is partly plausible. A school that notices more may in fact be less negligent.

Yet the rise in serious cases blocks any easy reassurance. Ministry materials released in `2025` warned that about `40` percent of serious incidents had not been recognized as bullying before they escalated into serious cases. That detail is hard to ignore. It suggests that the central difficulty is still the same old one: interactions that adults dismiss as horseplay, ordinary conflict, or a personality clash may already be harming a child deeply. By the time the institution catches up, attendance may have broken down, self-harm risk may have risen, and trust may already be gone.

The problem has also spilled beyond the classroom walls. `Netto ijime`, online bullying, extends the school social order into the hours when children are supposedly home and safe. Messages, screenshots, anonymous posts, and short videos can keep a target under pressure long after the final bell. That makes the Japanese case especially revealing. Schools are still expected to be responsible, but the social arena in which bullying operates is now partly digital, partly domestic, and not fully under school control. Teachers, families, welfare professionals, and sometimes police all end up sharing pieces of the response. The result is that `ijime` has become a test of institutional sensitivity as much as a test of student behavior. A school that recognizes many cases may be signaling vigilance. A school that reports very few may be overlooking the quieter forms of harm that flourish in group settings. Japan's official discussion now lives inside that uncomfortable ambiguity, trying to encourage earlier recognition without pretending that recognition alone solves the problem.

Understanding `ijime` helps explain a tension inside Japanese school culture. Schools are good at producing tight daily communities, shared routines, and a strong sense that everyone should move together. Those qualities can support belonging. They can also make exclusion particularly punishing. When social life depends heavily on group acceptance, being isolated inside the group carries a distinctive force. The public response to bullying in Japan, the legal definitions, the surveys, the investigation procedures, shows a country trying to make relational harm visible and administratively legible.

That effort is unfinished, which is why the term remains so important. `Ijime` is not a sensational corner of school life. It is a test of whether institutions can hear distress before it becomes catastrophe. A phone screen lighting up after dark, an empty seat the next morning, a board of education report written too late: in Japan, those small signs belong to the same argument over what counts as harm and who is obliged to recognize it.
