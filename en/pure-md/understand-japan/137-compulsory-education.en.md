# Compulsory Education: Nine Years, and the Problem of Staying in School

On a weekday morning in Japan, the most reliable rhythm is the walk to school. Children in small groups move through quiet residential streets, backpacks bouncing, a safety flag held by an older student. At the gate, a teacher greets them by name, and the day begins with the familiar machinery of homeroom, attendance, and rules. The ritual is so ordinary that it can look like nature. In Japan, it is law.

`Compulsory Education（義務教育）` refers to the nine years of ordinary schooling that Japan treats as a baseline right and a baseline obligation. The Ministry of Education’s explanations make the structure explicit: `6` years of elementary school and `3` years of lower secondary school, generally for children ages `6` through `15`. Guardians have a duty to ensure attendance, and the national and local governments have a duty to provide opportunity and maintain standards.

The legal skeleton is deliberately firm. Japan’s Constitution (`Article 26`) establishes the right to receive education and places the responsibility of schooling on those who have children in their care. The Basic Act on Education and the School Education Act then turn that principle into a system: municipalities are responsible for establishing the necessary public elementary and lower secondary schools, and public schools do not charge tuition for compulsory education. This is not merely a cultural preference for schooling. It is a state commitment to universal infrastructure—build the schools, staff them, and keep the minimum standard national.

Yet compulsory education is also where Japan’s administrative clarity meets everyday ambiguity. “Free” does not mean costless. The system does not charge tuition in public compulsory schools, but families routinely pay for lunch, supplies, learning materials, and—depending on the school—uniform-related costs and activities. The promise is not that education will be an expense-free experience; it is that tuition itself will not be the barrier that divides children into those who can attend and those who cannot.

The machinery that keeps the system national is easy to overlook because it is designed to be boring. Compulsory education depends on rules about class organization and staffing, on teacher allocation formulas, and on public finance that lets small municipalities run schools with a degree of uniformity. MEXT’s materials on class size, teacher numbers, and small-class education exist because “equal education” is not only a moral claim; it is a staffing chart and a budget line. It is one reason Japanese education can feel simultaneously local—each school a small community—and standardized, because the state is constantly balancing the freedom of municipalities with the obligation to provide a consistent baseline.

Compulsory education in Japan is a promise made to children as much as a requirement imposed on parents.

In recent years, the pressure point has shifted. Japan’s compulsory education is not struggling with “coverage” in the old sense; the country has schools. The more disturbing question is whether children can remain inside the schooling model the state has built. The Ministry of Education’s surveys on problem behavior and non-attendance show that `futōkō（不登校）`—students who do not attend school—has risen to record levels, with the `2025` release of the `FY2024` survey reporting roughly `354,000` non-attending elementary and lower secondary students. When numbers reach that scale, non-attendance stops being a marginal issue and becomes a systemic stress test. It forces the system to clarify what it is really promising: a seat in a classroom, or sustained access to learning. It also changes the moral tone of “obligation,” because obligation without a workable pathway becomes a form of abandonment.

Japan’s response has been to treat compulsory education less as a command and more as a guarantee of learning connection. Official materials now emphasize countermeasures: in-school support centers, alternative settings, remote learning, and mechanisms to keep children attached to education even when they cannot tolerate the standard classroom. These are not fringe measures; they are increasingly treated as part of the compulsory system’s own toolkit. This is a subtle shift in meaning. The duty remains, but the system increasingly admits that “school” is not always a building, and that attendance alone is not the same as education.

Compulsory education therefore reveals a central contradiction in contemporary Japan. The country relies on standardized institutions to deliver equality—same years, same baseline, same promise. Yet those institutions can become brittle under social change: smaller families, rising mental health pressures, and the diversification of childhood experiences. The legal framework is stable, but the lived reality is more variable. When a child cannot go to school, the system has to decide whether it will treat that as a failure of the child, a failure of the family, or a failure of the institution.

To understand Japan through `Compulsory Education（義務教育）` is to see both the strength and the new fragility of the country’s postwar social model. Nine years of schooling remains a national backbone: a shared pathway that helps produce literacy, social coordination, and a common civic baseline. The rising problem is no longer how to make children enter the system, but how to keep them inside learning when the system’s standard shape no longer fits everyone. The system is being asked to keep its universal promise while tolerating more ways of being a student than the old model assumed. That is a governance challenge as much as an educational one, because it forces Japan to redefine what “compulsory” guarantees.
