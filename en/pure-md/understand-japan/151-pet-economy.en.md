# Pet Economy: When Pets Become Family and the State Starts Tracking Them

The dog does not know it has an ID number. The owner does. A small chip sits under the skin; an online registry stores the information; the paperwork turns a private relationship into a public record. This is a quiet but telling detail of contemporary Japan. `Pet Economy（寵物經濟）` is not only about cute products and a booming market. It is the larger system that forms when dogs and cats move from the yard into the living room—when “pet” becomes “family,” and family starts to require governance.

In Japan, the pet economy is best understood as a chain rather than a single industry. It runs through `Pet Food（寵物食品）`, supplies, veterinary medicine, grooming, insurance, training, end-of-life services, disaster preparedness, and information registration. The point is not only consumption. It is that a household now organizes time, emotion, and recurring costs around an animal treated as a life companion. In an aging and increasingly single society, pets can function as emotional support, daily rhythm, and a substitute relationship—while also creating responsibilities that look, structurally, like caregiving.

Affection, in this system, becomes a monthly budget line. A chip, a checkup, a premium, a grooming appointment: the household calendar begins to look like care work.

The long-term background is demographic and cultural change. Postwar economic growth, urbanization, and housing patterns pushed pet-keeping away from purely utilitarian roles like guarding and pest control and toward indoor companionship. From the 1990s onward, as late marriage, non-marriage, and low birthrates deepened, the “family member” framing grew more prominent, and the market expanded far beyond basic food and leashes. A pet’s life could now be broken into serviceable stages—puppy or kitten care, health maintenance, illness management, aging support, and ultimately memorial arrangements.

Japan’s regulatory state followed that shift. On June 1, 2009, the `Pet Food Safety Law` (`愛がん動物用飼料の安全性の確保に関する法律`) took effect, moving pet food into a supervised category with requirements for manufacturing, import, sales, labeling, and recordkeeping. On June 1, 2022, Japan began implementing a microchip information registration制度 for dogs and cats. These dates matter because they mark a broader change in social meaning: pets are no longer treated only as private property. They are treated as beings whose safety, traceability, and owner responsibility require formal systems.

The market scale makes the transformation legible. The Pet Food Association’s distribution survey reports that in 2024 Japan’s pet food market reached 459,423 million yen (about 459.4 billion yen). The breakdown is also revealing: dog food totaled 186,339 million yen while cat food totaled 260,469 million yen, meaning the cat market had clearly surpassed the dog market. This fits an urban reality—smaller living spaces, different care patterns—and it shows that “pet economy” growth is driven by long-term care spending, not only by one-time purchases. Food is the anchor, but the long tail is services: clinic visits, chronic-disease management, senior care, and insurance that turns uncertainty into a predictable fee.

Governance is now built into the chain. MAFF and the Ministry of the Environment share responsibility for pet food safety, treating it as a managed safety product rather than a casual commodity. The microchip registration制度 creates a traceable line that links breeders and pet shops to owners and, in principle, makes abandonment, loss, and responsibility easier to investigate. The same logic appears in broader animal welfare administration. Japan is trying to hold two goals at once: allow a large consumer market to flourish and impose a minimum structure of welfare and accountability on the market’s most problematic edges.
For dogs and cats sold through businesses, the system is designed so identification begins with the seller, not just with the household that falls in love.

This is where the contemporary tensions concentrate. As services and quality rise, the cost of “proper” pet care rises too—turning companionship into a class-marked consumer behavior. The pet economy can soften loneliness in a super-aging society, yet it can also expose fragility: an older owner may love an animal deeply and still become unable to pay for medical care or provide daily support. Commercial breeding and sales can produce impulsive purchases, welfare problems, and downstream abandonment. In a disaster-prone country, evacuation and shelter policies also become pet policy, because animals cannot be left behind without consequences for both humans and animals.

Industry surveys reflect that widening scope. When a national association’s “pet-keeping reality” survey treats disaster preparedness, microchips, and support services as separate tracked categories, it is acknowledging that pet-keeping has become an infrastructure issue. The household may be the unit of affection, but the society must manage the externalities: public health, safety, shelter, and responsibility when the owner cannot continue.

Seen as a lens on Japan, the pet economy is not a soft cultural curiosity. It is a map of how demographic structure reshapes daily life and how the state responds when private choices become widespread social conditions. Japan did not simply decide to love pets more. It built a system—markets, services, and regulation—around a new kind of family member. The microchip, the safety law, the booming food market, and the expanding service ecosystem all point to the same conclusion: in contemporary Japan, companionship is both intimate and institutional.
