# Cold War Regime: The Postwar Order That Never Fully Left

In 1960, crowds filled the streets around the National Diet in Tokyo to protest the revised U.S.-Japan Security Treaty. The images from the Anpo protests are noisy, dense, and visibly political, but they also point to something larger than a single treaty fight. By then Japan's postwar order was already taking shape: security tied to the United States, economic recovery given priority over large-scale rearmament, conservative political dominance consolidating at home, and the constitution standing as both restraint and argument. The term `Cold War Regime` names that whole arrangement.

It began to harden in the first half of the 1950s. The 1951 San Francisco Peace Treaty ended the occupation framework and restored Japanese sovereignty in a limited, structured way. Signed alongside it was the original U.S.-Japan Security Treaty, which made clear that restored sovereignty would operate inside an American-led strategic order. Then, in 1955, the creation of the Liberal Democratic Party helped lock in the domestic side of the system. What later became known as the `1955 System`—long conservative rule facing an entrenched but oppositional left—fit neatly with the external Cold War environment. Internal order and external alignment reinforced one another.

Stability had a sponsor, and also a price for autonomy, burden, dependence, and argument.

The practical bargain was straightforward enough. Japan could keep defense spending relatively restrained, avoid the full political burden of becoming a major military power again, and concentrate national resources on economic recovery and industrial growth. In return, the country accepted a durable alliance structure centered on U.S. forces and the American security umbrella. This is the logic often associated with the Yoshida line after Prime Minister Shigeru Yoshida, though the historical reality was more contested than the formula sometimes implies. Still, the exchange was real. Security dependence bought space for growth, and growth helped legitimize the political order built around that dependence. The alliance was not a background condition. It was part of the operating design of postwar Japan.

That is why the Cold War regime is so useful for understanding postwar Japan. It links together things that are too often discussed separately: the U.S. alliance, Article 9 debates, the rise of conservative one-party predominance, export-led growth, and the persistent sense that foreign policy and domestic order are not fully separable. Once the system is seen as a whole, apparently disconnected issues begin to line up. A base dispute in Okinawa, a constitutional argument in Tokyo, and a security warning about Northeast Asia all sit on the same historical floorboards.

The arrangement also shaped political emotion. For supporters, it delivered peace, prosperity, and a clear place in the postwar world. For critics, it limited autonomy, narrowed debate, and placed a heavy share of the alliance burden on local communities, especially in Okinawa. The 1960 protests mattered because they showed that the regime's stability was never identical with consensus. Large parts of society accepted the order pragmatically without necessarily embracing it wholeheartedly. A structure can be durable and contested at the same time. Japan's Cold War order was exactly that.

Its endurance after the end of the Cold War is what makes the term especially revealing today. The Soviet Union disappeared, but the alliance remained. U.S. bases remained. The constitutional dispute remained. Strategic concern about China and the Korean Peninsula continued to keep security at the center of national debate. The Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Ministry of Defense still describe Japan's security environment through a language of alliance coordination and regional threat that feels like an updated extension of the old framework rather than a clean departure from it. Recent diplomacy may be framed through the `Free and Open Indo-Pacific`, deterrence, and economic security, but the institutional bedrock is recognizably older. Even the vocabulary of change has usually entered through structures built during the earlier order.

That continuity does not mean nothing changed. Japan has gradually expanded the scope of its international security role, revised legal interpretations, strengthened defense planning, and talked more openly about deterrence than it once did. Yet even those changes have tended to occur within the alliance structure rather than against it. The postwar order did not vanish; it adapted. That is why the phrase `Cold War regime` remains useful. It reminds us that the key issue is not whether the calendar says the Cold War is over. The key issue is whether the political architecture built under that pressure still organizes choices now. In Japan, it plainly does.

The barbed wire outside a U.S. base, the parliamentary debate over constitutional limits, the annual security documents, the memory of 1960 in political history: these belong to the same story. Japan's Cold War regime matters because it was never only a period label. It was a structure for distributing risk, legitimacy, and restraint across state and society. The superpower rivalry that helped build it is gone. Much of the order it produced is still standing, and much of today's security debate still speaks through its frame, even when the language now points to new threats and new theatres in East Asia and beyond.
