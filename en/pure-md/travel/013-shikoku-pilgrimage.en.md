# Shikoku Pilgrimage: The Long Way Around an Island

At some point on the road between temples in Tokushima, the souvenirs thin out, the bus stops grow infrequent, and the trip stops feeling like an excursion. You are carrying water, checking the next turn, and noticing your own pace in a way city travel rarely requires. This is usually when the Shikoku Pilgrimage begins to make sense. Known as the henro, it follows 88 temples across the four prefectures of Shikoku and traces the memory of Kobo Daishi, the monk Kukai, whose presence still shapes the route. It is one of those Japanese journeys that can be entered as religion, exercise, curiosity, or fatigue management, and yet it asks something bodily of everyone.

Officially, the scale is plain enough. The Association of the Shikoku Pilgrimage Temples frames the route through 88 sacred sites, and JNTO describes it as a 1,200-kilometer circuit that many walkers take about six weeks to cover on foot. Modern travelers can compress it by car or bus, and some do it in sections over several years, but the route resists the logic of quick collecting. Even when broken into manageable pieces, it remains a chain of temple gates, roadside climbs, quiet town centers, and practical questions about where to sleep, when to refill a bottle, and how to get back to a station before dark.

What makes the pilgrimage specifically Japanese is not only the Buddhist frame or the age of the temples. It is the way faith, local life, and travel infrastructure still overlap. Pilgrims wear white jackets, carry stamp books, recite sutras, or simply bow and move on, but the route is also threaded through convenience stores, minshuku, highway edges, and ordinary neighborhoods. The idea of `osettai`, the unsolicited kindness shown to pilgrims, matters here. A cup of tea, a snack, directions, or a short word from a resident turns the road into a social landscape rather than a solitary test. That mixture of ritual form and everyday hospitality is hard to separate from the experience itself.

For travelers, the importance of the henro lies in how completely it changes trip logic. A normal Japan itinerary rewards efficiency: early trains, tightly grouped sights, and the neat accomplishment of moving from city to city. The pilgrimage rewards repetition and patience. You think in temple numbers, walking hours, elevation, and laundry opportunities. A day that includes only three or four temples can feel full. A stretch of rain can reorder the entire plan. Even a short two-day introduction, like the route JNTO proposes in Tokushima, teaches a different discipline from ordinary sightseeing because it makes distance and sequence more important than spectacle.

That is why the first useful expectation correction is this: the Shikoku Pilgrimage is not best approached as an endurance stunt or a grand checklist. Some first-time visitors imagine that "doing the pilgrimage" means all 88 temples in one push, preferably on foot, preferably with stoic silence. In practice, Japan's own tourism and temple sources present several valid ways in. Walking the whole circuit is one tradition, but section walking, taxi-assisted segments, bus-based routes, and short introductory pilgrimages are all recognized ways of entering the road. The difference is less about authenticity than about whether your body, weather window, and schedule can sustain the rhythm you have chosen.

A second friction point is etiquette. Because the route is now famous, some travelers arrive expecting a scenic trail with temples attached. What they meet instead is a living religious corridor. There are worship procedures, temple offices with specific hours, halls where voices naturally drop, and lodging options that may be basic rather than picturesque. White clothing and staffs are not costumes. Stamp books are not theme-park passports. Even photography needs judgment. The pilgrimage can be welcoming to nonbelievers, but it does not stop being devotional ground simply because a traveler entered through a guide article rather than a family tradition.

The itinerary consequences are immediate. If you are walking, where you stay matters as much as which temple you visit, because long rural sections can leave little margin after late afternoon. If you are driving, you still need to think about temple closing times and the way mountain roads slow movement. Spring and autumn tend to be easier on the body than midsummer, while winter can shorten daylight and complicate less urban stretches. Good shoes, a light pack, cash for smaller lodgings or offerings, and a realistic plan for transport back from the final temple of the day all matter more here than in most mainstream Japanese trips.

There is also the question of what kind of record you want to keep. Many pilgrims carry a `nokyocho`, the temple stamp book that collects calligraphy and seals at each stop, and that object subtly changes the trip. It turns every temple office into a point of pause and reminds the traveler that progress is not measured only in distance. Others wear the traditional white vest or carry the wooden staff associated with the route. None of this is compulsory for a modern visitor, but the material culture matters because it slows the eye and gives shape to the repetition. The pilgrimage is full of such small objects: a bell rope, a name slip, a coin tray, a pair of shoes left outside a hall.

The henro remains one of the clearest ways to understand how Japanese travel can still be structured by pilgrimage rather than tourism alone. It is not pure asceticism, and it is not a museum piece. It is an old route that has learned to live beside rental cars, official model itineraries, and contemporary curiosity without becoming entirely modernized. By the time you leave, what stays in the mind is often not a single grand temple but a sequence of ordinary details: a stamp drying on a page, a roadside vending machine, wet shoes outside a lodging door, and the sense that the island has been teaching pace as much as place.
