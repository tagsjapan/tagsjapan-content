# Social Stratification: The Inequality Beneath Japan's Middle-Class Surface

In Japan, class difference is often easier to detect in schedules and options than in dramatic visual contrast. Who can afford cram school without crisis. Who can turn down a bad shift. Who can move for a better job. Who can take time off to care for a parent without wrecking the household budget. `Social Stratification` in Japan is not just a matter of rich and poor standing far apart. It is the structure that sorts people by income, job security, education, family background, and regional opportunity long before the sorting has to call itself class.

Inequality in Japan often arrives quietly.

That quietness is part of the story. For decades, Japan described itself, and was often described by others, as a broadly middle-class society. Stable employment, rising postwar incomes, educational expansion, and the authority of company-centered life made that image plausible for many people. Yet the long aftermath of the bubble collapse, the spread of nonregular employment, aging, regional decline, and changing family forms have made the old image harder to sustain. The country did not stop being orderly. It stopped being equally buffered by the order.

Official data makes the change hard to dismiss. The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's `2022` Comprehensive Survey of Living Conditions reported a `relative poverty rate` of `15.4%` and a `child poverty rate` of `11.5%`. The most striking number in the same release may be the contrast within working-age households with children: households headed by one adult faced a poverty rate of `44.5%`, while households with two or more adults stood at `8.6%`. Those figures show how stratification in Japan is carried not only by wages but by family structure, care burden, and the fragility of relying on a single income in a society with high daily costs.

Japan has measured these differences for a long time, even when public language lagged behind. The `SSM` surveys on social stratification and mobility have tracked class and mobility since `1955`, with the `2015` survey explicitly focusing on changes under low fertility and aging. That research tradition matters because it undercuts the idea that inequality is merely a recent mood or imported rhetoric. Japanese society has long known how to study class. What changed is that more of the evidence now escapes specialist circles and enters policy, media, and ordinary anxiety.

The structure is built through several linked channels. Employment remains decisive because the gap between secure regular work and insecure nonregular work shapes income, insurance, marriage prospects, and retirement security. Family structure matters because divorce, solo parenting, and caregiving responsibilities do not distribute risk evenly. Education matters because entrance exams, tutoring, university costs, and the ability to delay earning in favor of study all depend partly on family resources. Regional conditions matter because the range of available schools, jobs, and transport differs sharply between metropolitan cores and thinner local economies. Stratification is durable because these channels reinforce one another. A household that can absorb tutoring fees, entrance-exam failures, or a move to a larger city buys time. A household without that margin begins making smaller choices earlier, and those smaller choices accumulate into class difference over a lifetime.

Education policy shows that the state now recognizes some of this more openly. The Ministry of Education's higher education support system began in `2020`, and by `2025` tuition and entrance-fee reductions for students from families with three or more dependent children had been extended up to a nationally defined amount without income limits. That expansion is more than a technical subsidy. It is a quiet admission that educational opportunity cannot be left entirely to household finances if Japan wants to speak seriously about mobility. When the state intervenes in tuition at that scale, it is acknowledging that starting points are not equal.

Still, the language of self-responsibility remains powerful. Japan has become more willing to talk about poverty, children's hardship, and unequal opportunity, but it often continues to narrate difficulty as a problem of personal planning, family resilience, or individual effort. This is where the contradiction of stratification appears most clearly. The country produces meticulous data and policy responses while retaining a moral vocabulary that can shift attention away from structural causes. Support exists, yet the idea that people should manage risk within the family or through personal discipline still carries great force. The result is a society that can acknowledge inequality statistically while continuing to imagine fairness morally.

`Social Stratification` matters in Japan because it explains the gap between the country's smooth public surface and the uneven distribution of security underneath it. The station is clean, the service is precise, the routines are stable, and many lives remain tightly budgeted against illness, school costs, unstable work, or care obligations. The old image of middle-class sameness has not vanished entirely, but it no longer describes the whole field. To understand class in Japan is to notice how much depends on whether a person has enough margin to choose, recover, and keep going when the system stops being generous. Class often appears in the distance between a setback and a household's ability to absorb it.
